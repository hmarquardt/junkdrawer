Is China trying to slow U.S. AI?

There is real evidence of a China-linked influence operation targeting American arguments about AI infrastructure. That is not the same thing as proving the AI-safety or “decel” movement is a Chinese psyop.

The clean answer: evidence of a targeted operation — yes. Evidence that “AI decel” itself is a Chinese operation — no.
EVIDENCED
Likely PRC-origin operators posed as Americans and generated anti-data-center messaging for U.S. audiences. 1
NOT EVIDENCED
That American AI-safety researchers, environmental opponents, or the broader deceleration movement are controlled by China.

The 30-second version

In June 2026, OpenAI disclosed two clusters of accounts it said likely originated in China. One generated English social posts and images arguing that AI data centers raise electricity demand and household costs. The operators posed as Americans and used likely inauthentic social accounts. 2

01 / TARGET

AI data centers

The campaign focused on a real U.S. political pressure point: electricity prices, grid demand and local impacts from data-center construction.

02 / METHOD

Fake “ordinary Americans”

OpenAI says operators requested content from the perspective of U.S. workers, students, mothers, clerks, investors and other personas. 1

03 / IMPACT

Little evidence it worked

OpenAI said it found no meaningful breakout beyond the operation's own activity. In other words: real attempt, unproven persuasion. 2

What actually happened?

OpenAI's “Data Center Bandwagon” case gives us an unusually direct view into the workflow because the operators used ChatGPT as part of the operation.

1. Find a real grievancePower prices, grid strain, local development.
2. Build believable personasAccounts that appear American and locally grounded.
3. Generate contentEnglish comments, images and talking points.
4. Post & amplifyLikely inauthentic social accounts, cross-account interaction.
5. Look organicBlend into a legitimate debate already happening.
The important bit: an influence operation does not need to invent a fake problem. It can be more effective to take a real concern and selectively amplify it while hiding who is doing the amplifying.
“The operation sought to exploit and amplify existing public concerns about energy prices and local impacts of data center development.” OpenAI, June 10, 2026 2

This tactic has a history

The data-center operation is more interesting because it resembles an older pattern: covertly posing as locals to oppose U.S. projects in strategically important industries.

2019 →
DRAGONBRIDGE emerges

Mandiant and Google track a large PRC-linked influence network operating across social platforms. Google later described it as the most prolific PRC-linked influence actor it tracks. 3

2022
Rare-earth projects targeted

DRAGONBRIDGE accounts posed as concerned Texas residents and pushed environmental and health fears around a planned rare-earth processing facility — a project that would compete with China's strategic market position. 4

2024
Massive account disruption continues

Google said it disrupted more than 10,000 instances of DRAGONBRIDGE activity in Q1 2024 and more than 175,000 over the network's lifetime at that point, while noting engagement was generally poor. 3

2026
AI infrastructure becomes the target

OpenAI discloses PRC-origin activity testing narratives against data centers, electricity prices, tariffs and U.S.-China technology competition. 2 5

Don't make the opposite mistake

Discovering propaganda around an issue does not make everyone who shares that opinion a propagandist.

These can be legitimate concerns

  • Electricity prices
  • Grid capacity
  • Water use
  • Noise and land use
  • Tax incentives
  • AI safety and job disruption

These are influence-operation behaviors

  • Fake identities and personas
  • Hidden foreign coordination
  • Astroturfing local opposition
  • Mass-produced talking points
  • Coordinated amplification
  • Deliberately disguising the sponsor

The useful takeaway for normal humans

You do not have to dismiss criticism of AI infrastructure. You should become more skeptical when a sudden wave of identical, emotionally charged local outrage appears online — especially when the accounts seem generic, recently created, oddly prolific, or disconnected from the communities they claim to represent.

The information-operation question is not “Is this opinion allowed?” It is “Who is speaking, are they real, and are they hiding coordination?”

So… is this a “psyop”?

In everyday language, yes, there is evidence of a China-linked covert influence operation touching the AI-decel ecosystem. In the stronger conspiratorial sense — “China created or controls the American AI-safety movement” — the evidence simply is not there.

The defensible statement is narrower — and more interesting:

China-linked influence operators have identified American skepticism toward AI infrastructure as a useful attack surface and have actively tried to amplify it.
