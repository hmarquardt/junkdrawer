A field guide to neighborhood radio

What if your neighborhood could text without cell towers?

No cell service. No internet. No monthly subscription. No ham radio license required for compliant U.S. devices. Just small radios, neighbors, and a network we build together.

See how it works How do I join?

Short text messages, sent radio to radio. Not phone calls, not the internet — something smaller and, in the right moment, more useful.

01 · An ordinary Tuesday

The infrastructure under your message

A summer storm rolls through. The power goes out. Your phone still has a charge, but service comes and goes, and nothing on the internet answers.

You want to check on a neighbor a mile away. Ordinarily, that one small message depends on somebody else’s infrastructure: your phone, the nearest cell tower, fiber under a road, a company’s servers, another tower, their phone. It works so well that most of us have never had to picture it.

A neighborhood radio network changes the picture. Instead of one strong signal reaching a tower miles away, a handful of small radios send modest signals to each other — and pass messages along. No tower. No account. No monthly bill. The radios belong to the people using them.

This is not a replacement for the internet, and it will not make a phone call. It is something narrower and, in the right moment, genuinely useful: short text messages that travel over the air between people you know.

What we’re talking about

  • ✓Short text messages, including group channels and direct messages.
  • ✓Position sharing on radios that include GPS.
  • ✓No cell service or internet needed for local messaging.
  • ✓Community-owned — the equipment is yours, not a subscription.

What it isn’t

  • ✕Not voice calls, video, or general internet access.
  • ✕Not guaranteed delivery, and not a substitute for 911 or satellite SOS.
  • ✕Not a private phone network — privacy depends on how it’s configured.

What is happening

Loading the scene…

    Dashed lines show which radios can hear each other in this drawing. Real coverage depends on terrain, buildings, antennas, and settings — this is not a map of your town.

    What the three situations show

    A — Direct. Two radios close enough to hear each other. One transmission, no helpers required.

    B — With help. The houses are too far apart for a single jump. A radio in between hears the message, repeats it, and the far end receives it. The people in the middle didn’t do anything except leave their radio on.

    C — More neighbors. A relay placed high on a water tower bridges a gap that ordinary rooftops can’t cross. Take it away and the two sides of the neighborhood each stay connected locally, but the message can’t get across.

    Two real systems, two styles of helping

    This drawing smooths over an honest difference. In Meshtastic, compatible nodes can forward traffic according to the mesh’s forwarding rules, so ordinary radios often do the passing. In MeshCore, relaying commonly comes from designated repeaters and room servers rather than from every phone-connected companion radio.

    Both approaches produce the same experience for the person holding the phone: type a message, and it may reach someone who is out of direct range. Neither guarantees that it will.

    Real networks also cap how many times a message may repeat — Meshtastic’s default allows three hops — and more hops means more airtime and more chances for collisions.

    03 · Where your phone fits

    Your phone is still the interface

    You probably won’t type on the radio at all. In the most common setup, a small radio does the long-distance work and your ordinary phone stays in your hand.

    The radio pairs with your phone over Bluetooth. That link is short — the same few meters as wireless earbuds. A messaging app on the phone sends your text to the radio beside you.

    The radio then transmits over LoRa, a low-power radio technique built for small amounts of data over long distances. Your message crosses the neighborhood as radio, not as cellular data. Other radios may carry it further. At the other end, a radio hands the text to the recipient’s phone over Bluetooth again.

    None of that requires a cell tower, a Wi-Fi network, or an internet connection. It also means the experience feels familiar: a contact list, a message thread, a send button. The unfamiliar part stays in the little box on your bag.

    🔌

    Some radios don’t need a phone at all. Handheld devices with built-in screens and keyboards — and many solar repeater nodes — run on their own. The phone is a convenience, not a requirement.

    1. 📱 Your phoneThe keyboard, screen, and messaging app you already know. Bluetooth
    2. ↓
    3. 📻 Your radioA small LoRa device on your bag, desk, or windowsill. This is the part that travels. Radio
    4. ↓
    5. 🏘️ Other radiosNeighbors’ nodes, or a relay up high, passing the message along as needed. Radio
    6. ↓
    7. 📥 Their radioReceives the text and hands it to the phone in their pocket. Bluetooth

    Bluetooth is short-range and local. LoRa does the distance. Cellular data is not involved at any point.

    ☀️

    Set it up on a sunny day. Apps, firmware, maps, and documentation generally have to be downloaded while you still have internet. A radio you configured last month is a backup plan; a radio still in its box is a project. Charge it, update it, and send a test message before you need it.

    04 · Why bother?

    Six good reasons to keep a radio in the kitchen drawer

    You don’t need to be preparing for the end of the world to appreciate a backup plan for an ordinary Tuesday.

    🌩️

    Storms and power outages

    Coordinate with family or neighbors when familiar services are unavailable — and when everyone is suddenly trying to use the same cell tower at once.

    🌾

    Rural communities

    Exchange local messages in places where cell reception is spotty, expensive, or simply absent once you leave the highway.

    🏕️

    Camping and the outdoors

    Keep a group in touch when they spread out beyond ordinary coverage: the trailhead, the ridge, the other side of the lake.

    🎪

    Festivals and community events

    Coordinate volunteers, parking crews, and organizers without depending entirely on congested cellular networks at the busiest hour.

    🏘️

    Neighborhood resilience

    Build a communications resource maintained by the people who benefit from it — with a handful of radios and a shared set of expectations.

    🔬

    Because it’s interesting

    Experiment with a community-powered network that costs little to operate, and learn how radio actually behaves in your own streets.

    📞

    An honest caveat about emergencies. These systems are not guaranteed emergency communications. They do not replace 911, satellite SOS devices, or other emergency services, and they should never be your only plan when someone’s safety is at stake. Treat them as an additional layer — useful for coordination, not a substitute for calling for help.

    05 · Meet the two

    Meshtastic and MeshCore

    Two open-source ecosystems, one broad goal: small radios, no towers, and messages that can travel farther when more people participate.

    What they have in common

    • ✓Off-grid text messaging
    • ✓Affordable, low-power LoRa hardware
    • ✓Community participation
    • ✓Potentially long range, with suitable terrain and placement
    • ✓No ordinary cellular or internet service required
    • ✓No recurring cellular data subscription
    • ✓Encryption features, subject to configuration and system limits
    • ✓Open-source firmware and an active community
    Decentralized mesh

    Meshtastic

    An open-source ecosystem built around affordable LoRa radios that form a decentralized mesh. Compatible nodes can relay traffic according to the system’s forwarding rules, so ordinary radios often pass messages along without a dedicated repeater.

    • Phones connect to a radio over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, or USB.
    • Many device types, from pocket nodes to solar boards to handhelds with keyboards.
    • Channels are encrypted; the default public channel is shared by anyone using it.
    • Large community, extensive documentation, active regional groups.

    meshtastic.org — introduction · supported hardware

    Companions + repeaters

    MeshCore

    An open-source ecosystem also built on LoRa, with a stronger distinction between personal companion radios and purpose-built repeaters that extend the network. Companion radios do not repeat traffic; designated repeaters and room servers do.

    • Companion radios pair with an app on your phone or computer.
    • Repeaters are meant for high, well-placed locations — often solar powered.
    • Messages are encrypted, with public and private channels.
    • Some handhelds run standalone firmware with no phone required.

    meshcore.io · docs.meshcore.io

    Both projects are moving targets. The comparison above describes the general design each is known for, not a promise about any particular firmware version.

    The better system is usually the one nearby

    Neither ecosystem automatically wins on range, battery life, reliability, security, or routing. Those depend far more on your terrain, your antenna, your settings, and how many people around you are actually on the air.

    A radio that can hear nobody is a radio that does nothing. If there’s a Meshtastic group two towns over and no MeshCore users within thirty miles, the interesting question has already been answered for you.

    One radio, one firmware at a time

    Some hardware can run either ecosystem, but a device typically runs one firmware at a time. Switching means reflashing and reconfiguring — not flipping a switch in an app.

    That’s a practical argument for asking around before you buy: choose the network you can actually join, and leave the other ecosystem for your second radio or your next experiment.

    06 · The part everyone asks about

    Wait — I don’t need a radio license?

    In the United States, appropriately configured, compliant devices can operate in the 902–928 MHz band under FCC Part 15 rules, without an amateur radio license. No exam. No call sign. No fee.

    Part 15 is the same family of rules that lets Wi-Fi routers, cordless phones, and garage door openers exist. The equipment is expected to be certified, to stay within power and antenna limits, and to tolerate interference from other users of the band. The projects publish region-specific settings for a reason: picking the wrong region, power setting, or frequency can put you outside what’s permitted.

    A license-free band does not mean every possible radio configuration is legal. A compliant radio, correctly configured, for your country, is the whole trick.

    You don’t need to become a ham radio operator to get started.

    • ✓No amateur exam for ordinary Part 15 operation of compliant devices.
    • ✓Equipment matters. Certified hardware, proper antenna, and legal power limits.
    • ✓Configuration matters. Select your region and use the settings the project recommends.
    • ✕Amateur modes are different. Licensed operation has its own rules — including no encryption and identifying with your call sign.
    • 🌍Rules vary by country. Bands, power limits, and duty cycles differ. Check local guidance before transmitting.

    Read the primary sources: 47 CFR Part 15 (especially §15.247 for the 902–928 MHz band), Meshtastic’s region guide and legal notes, and MeshCore’s FAQ on setting a legal frequency for your country.

    07 · How far can it reach?

    Farther than you’d guess. Shorter than you’d hope.

    Anyone who quotes you a single number is describing their best day, in their best location. Distance is a property of the whole situation — not the box.

    Dense neighborhood

    Rooftops, walls, and trees absorb and scatter the signal. Expect shorter, less predictable hops and a network that benefits from more intermediate radios.

    No distances appear here on purpose. Any honest estimate needs the actual houses, hills, and antennas — and even then, radio is polite enough to surprise you.

    • ⛰️Terrain and hillsGround between you and the other radio is the biggest factor of all.
    • 🏢Buildings and vegetationWalls, roofs, and wet foliage all take something out of the signal.
    • 📡Antenna quality and positionA better antenna, outdoors and upright, often beats more transmit power.
    • ⬆️HeightA radio at roof level usually hears much farther than one at ground level.
    • 🎛️Radio settings and interferenceSpeed, bandwidth, and local noise change what’s possible at the same distance.
    • 🔁Relay availability and placementOne well-placed node can change everything; one missing node can split a network.
    🏔️

    About those spectacular long-distance stories. People have set records of hundreds of kilometers between two nodes — Meshtastic’s documentation lists a ground-to-ground record of 331 km. Those runs used elevated positions, carefully chosen antennas and settings, and a lot of patience. They are wonderful proof of what radio can do, and a poor guide to what your handheld will do on a Tuesday.

    08 · What does it cost?

    Three ways in, none of them a subscription

    Hardware prices move, and they vary enormously by country and by how much radio you want. So this page won’t quote a number. It will show you the shape of the spending.

    Path one

    Try it with a friend

    Two compatible, ready-to-use radios. No existing neighborhood network required — the two of you are the network. This is the cheapest way to find out whether the idea fits your life.

    Lowest commitment · works anywhere

    Path two

    Join a nearby network

    One compatible radio may be enough if participating nodes are already within usable range of your house, your trailhead, or your car. Ask before you buy which system and settings people are using.

    Best value · requires local coverage

    Path three

    Help build local coverage

    Participate in placing and maintaining a suitable relay or repeater — on a willing property, with permission, proper installation, and someone who will check on it. This is where a group becomes infrastructure.

    Highest impact · needs cooperation

    What the money actually goes to

    • 📻The radio itself, and a case or mount that survives your bag.
    • 📡A sensible antenna, cable, and a way to get it outdoors and upright.
    • 🔋Power: batteries, a charger, or a small solar setup for a relay.
    • 🔧Maintenance and replacement — weather is undefeated.
    • 🌐Optional internet-connected services, if you choose to use them. Not required for local messaging.

    The bill that isn’t there

    There is no monthly cellular subscription required for local off-grid messaging. Nobody meters your messages, and the network doesn’t stop working because a company changed a plan.

    That doesn’t make it free. You buy hardware, you charge batteries, you replace a cable after a storm, and someone has to climb the ladder. The difference is that the costs are visible, one-time, and shared — rather than invisible and recurring.

    Start with the projects’ own hardware documentation instead of a shopping list from a stranger: Meshtastic supported devices · MeshCore hardware. Check that a model supports your region’s frequency before ordering.

    09 · The chicken-and-egg problem

    What if nobody nearby has one?

    Fair question. A radio is considerably more interesting when there are people within reach to message.

    Here is the honest version: in many places, you will be the first person on your street with one of these. That is not a reason to skip it. It is the actual work.

    A local network can begin with just two participants. Two friends who can reach each other across a subdivision. A hiking group that splits up on weekends. A club, a volunteer auxiliary, a neighborhood association, a family spread across a few rural acres.

    From there it grows the way most useful local things grow: someone mentions it, someone else buys a radio, someone with a good roofline volunteers to host a relay. Nobody needs to cover a city. You need to cover the people you actually want to reach.

    That makes this less like buying a gadget and more like starting a small piece of community infrastructure — with all the ordinary complications that implies: permission, maintenance, expectations, and a shared understanding of what the network is for.

    The whole idea in one sentence

    The network doesn’t have to be everywhere. It just has to start somewhere.

    A caution about scale: adding nodes helps until it doesn’t. More participants can improve coverage and also add congestion, configuration mismatches, and maintenance. Meshes are not infinite, and a neighborhood-sized network is a perfectly respectable goal.

    1. Two friendsTwo radios, one conversation. Proof that it works.
    2. A few householdsA handful of neighbors on a shared channel, testing who can hear whom.
    3. A neighborhoodOne or two well-placed relays extend coverage across the whole area.
    4. Connected local groupsClubs, events, and neighboring communities share settings and expectations.

    10 · Start your own

    Five steps, one afternoon, two radios

    You don’t need to understand firmware, antennas, or packet routing to begin. You need a device that works out of the box, one other person, and a willingness to test.

    1. Find out whether a compatible community exists nearby.

      Look for local Meshtastic or MeshCore users before you spend anything. Community mesh maps, regional forums, and local radio or preparedness clubs are the usual starting points. Absence of evidence isn’t proof of absence — but it does tell you what to expect.

      Community mesh map (unofficial) · MeshCore map · Meshtastic community

    2. Decide which ecosystem makes sense locally.

      Ask what people around you are actually running, which channel and preset they use, and whether anyone maintains a repeater. If there is no local group, pick the ecosystem whose documentation and app you find easiest to follow — you are the local group now.

      Don’t publish or share channel keys publicly. Ask the group how they prefer to onboard new people.

    3. Obtain a supported, region-appropriate ready-to-use device.

      Buy hardware that is listed as supported, and make sure it is the variant intended for your country’s frequency band. A cheap board with the wrong band is not a bargain. Ready-to-use devices usually arrive with firmware installed; flashing is optional, not a rite of passage.

      Meshtastic supported devices · MeshCore devices & flasher

    4. Connect it to your phone, configure it, and send a test message.

      Pair over Bluetooth, install the app, set your region, choose the right channel, and give the radio a name your neighbors will recognize. Then send a message to yourself or a second radio. Test at increasing distance — down the street, around the corner, from the place you would actually want it to work.

      Meshtastic getting started · MeshCore app & setup docs

    5. Invite a friend or neighbor — and test again next month.

      A network of one is a hobby. A network of three is infrastructure. Show someone how it works, help them configure their radio, and agree on a periodic check-in so the equipment is charged, updated, and still working when you need it.

      Put a recurring reminder in your calendar. Batteries and good intentions both fade quietly.

    If you’re the technically curious type

    There is a second path, and it is genuinely valuable: contributing an elevated, solar-powered, continuously powered relay at a location with a good view of the neighborhood. That is usually the single biggest improvement available to a local network.

    It also involves real work — a weatherproof enclosure, a power budget, mounting, permission from the property owner, and someone willing to check on it. Do the first four steps before volunteering for this one. Nobody needs a repeater before anyone has a radio.

    Coordinate with the people who will use it. An inaccessible relay that fails silently in month three is worse than no relay at all.

    11 · What this technology cannot do

    The limitations are part of the deal

    A tool you understand is more useful than a tool you overestimate. Here is what these radios will not do, stated plainly.

    • ✕Not voice or internet. No phone calls, no video, no web browsing. Text, positions, and small bits of data.
    • ✕Not guaranteed delivery. A message can be lost to distance, terrain, interference, or congestion. There is no tower promising to try again.
    • ✕Limited bandwidth. Radios share a narrow channel. Busy networks get slow, and long messages or frequent position updates make it worse.
    • ✕Physical range limits. Hills, buildings, and trees win more often than marketing copy admits.
    • ✕Power dependence. Every radio needs a charged battery or steady power. A dead node is not a node.
    • ✕Compatibility requirements. Same ecosystem, compatible settings, right region, shared channel. Meshtastic and MeshCore do not talk to each other.
    • ✕Privacy depends on configuration. Encryption helps, but public channels are public. Do not treat any channel as confidential by default.
    • ✕Internet gateways are optional. Some setups bridge radio traffic to the internet. Convenient, but not required for local messaging — and not something to rely on during an outage.
    • ✕Not an emergency service. These networks are not a substitute for 911, satellite SOS, or professional emergency communications.
    • ✕Not zero maintenance. Firmware changes, batteries age, cables corrode, and the person who set it up may move away.

    None of this makes the technology useless. It makes it a tool with a shape — and a neighbor-built network that does one modest thing reliably is worth more than a grand plan that never gets switched on.

    12 · The network we build together

    We spent decades connecting ourselves to networks owned by someone else.

    Here’s a small opportunity to build one that belongs to the people using it. Start with two radios. Add a friend. Invite a neighbor. See how far a little cooperation can go.
