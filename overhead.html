ABOUT OVERHEAD

Know when to step outside.

Overhead tells you what satellites are actually worth going outside to see from a specific location. It combines real orbital geometry with real observing conditions — sunlight, darkness, weather and elevation — instead of just listing everything that crosses the sky.

What does Overhead watch?

  • The International Space Station and Tiangong, the two crewed stations.
  • Bright satellites from CelesTrak's "100 (or so) brightest" catalog.
  • Recent launches from the last 30 days, clearly labeled as brightness-uncertain.
  • Starlink trains — newly launched Starlink groups that still fly close enough together to appear as a line of moving lights. Overhead groups spacecraft by actual launch, checks whether they still form a coherent train from your location, and presents the whole formation as a single event with its own train score.

Overhead plans seven nights ahead, and the Best Thing This Week panel answers two separate questions: which single event is the best of the next seven nights, and whether it is genuinely exceptional or simply the best of an ordinary week.

What does “visible” mean?

A satellite is only worth looking for if more is true than simply being above the horizon. Overhead checks the pass elevation against your minimum, whether the spacecraft is actually in sunlight while your sky is dark (Sun 6° or more below the horizon), how long it stays usefully high, the object's brightness category, and the forecast cloud cover, visibility and precipitation at your location.

Buildings, trees, haze, spacecraft orientation and the limits of brightness modeling can still prevent a sighting. Overhead's answer is an honest recommendation, not a guarantee.

How does the score work?

Each event gets a 0–100 recommendation score from geometry (how high it climbs), useful duration, full sunlight on the spacecraft, darkness at your location, your forecast weather, and the object's brightness category. No precise apparent magnitude is claimed. When the weather forecast is missing, weather points are omitted and the score is marked provisional.

Starlink trains are scored separately: the number of simultaneously illuminated satellites, how tightly they are grouped (median spacing and apparent span), elevation, the strong-viewing-window length, darkness and weather. A train's age since deployment is shown as context; it is never the dominant scoring rule.

Best does not automatically mean exceptional. The weekly analysis compares every event across seven nights. An event is only called exceptional when it clears stricter criteria: near-elite geometry, clear weather, high prominence, fresh forecasts, and quality that stands apart from the rest of the week. A quiet week still has a best event — described honestly as such.

Starlink Train Watch

Right after a SpaceX launch, the deployed satellites fly in a tight line and can look like a moving "string of pearls." Over the following days they spread apart. Overhead groups Starlink spacecraft by their actual launch (international designator), prefers CelesTrak's supplemental elements computed from SpaceX's own ephemerides, propagates the whole cohort for your location, and measures whether the members are still close enough in your sky to read as a train. Cohorts are classified Fresh Train, Dispersing Train, Weak Train or Dispersed — only the first three are surfaced as events. If the orbital elements are stale, the score is capped and the event is labeled uncertain rather than pretending precision.

Your location and privacy

  • All orbital propagation, scoring and train detection run locally in your browser. No account, no API key.
  • Saved locations, favorites and preferences stay on this device (browser storage, up to 12 saved sites).
  • Your geographic coordinates are needed to calculate what passes overhead. Coordinates are sent to Open-Meteo for weather forecasts and timezone lookup, and to its geocoding service only when you search for a place. CelesTrak requests contain no information about you.
  • This page includes the repository's Analytics Lite page-view tracker. Map tiles load only when you open a map.

Where does the data come from?

  • CelesTrak — orbital elements (GP/OMM), the bright-satellite and recent-launch catalogs, and supplemental Starlink elements derived from SpaceX public ephemerides.
  • Open-Meteo — hourly weather forecasts, timezone resolution and place search.
  • satellite.js — local SGP4 orbital propagation.
  • SunCalc — sun and moon position calculations.
  • MapLibre — optional map rendering, loaded on demand with OpenStreetMap tiles.

How is this built?

Overhead is a single-page, client-side application in vanilla JavaScript. Orbital propagation runs in a background worker so the page stays responsive; scoring and train classification are deterministic — no AI or language model is involved in any recommendation. Source data is cached locally with explicit freshness tracking, and stale caches are recovered rather than silently trusted. Light and dark themes are both first-class.

Accuracy and limitations

  • Orbital element predictions degrade as data ages; newly deployed spacecraft can maneuver.
  • Brightness is partly heuristic — a catalog category is not a promise of naked-eye visibility, and apparent brightness depends on spacecraft orientation.
  • Clouds and haze can differ from the forecast; terrain, buildings and trees are not modeled.
  • Starlink deployments can change rapidly; train predictions are the most sensitive to element age.
  • Overhead is an observing aid for curious people. It is not an operational aerospace or navigation system.

How the recommendation works

The night’s score is the best remaining satellite opportunity among your selected categories. It is not a weather score. A “likely visible” segment needs at least 20 seconds above your minimum elevation, with the Sun at least 6° below the horizon and the spacecraft fully sunlit. Buildings, trees, haze and object orientation can still prevent a sighting.

Base points: elevation 25, useful visible duration 15, sunlight 10, observer darkness 15, brightness category 10, cloud cover 20, and visibility 5. Cloud, poor visibility and rain then reduce the total. Unknown weather earns no weather points and makes the result provisional. No precise apparent magnitude is claimed.
