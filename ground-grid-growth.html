The one-minute version

  1. Data centers can create serious local power, water, land, noise, and fiscal consequences.
  2. “Data center” describes facilities with dramatically different designs and impacts.
  3. A rational farm sale does not automatically justify rezoning or public incentives.Public decision requires project evidence
  4. A large announced investment is not the same as a large net public benefit.
  5. The site, utility contract, cooling design, public agreement, and enforcement terms matter more than whether the project carries an AI label.Project-dependent conclusion
This project does not ask you to support data centers. It asks you to understand the land, farming economy, infrastructure, and public deal well enough to oppose or support a specific proposal for defensible reasons.

Why I made this

I built this project out of curiosity, not professional authority. I am not a farmer or an expert in agriculture, utilities, or land-use planning. I kept encountering strong claims about farmland, farming, data centers, water, electricity, and economic development—and realized I did not understand the subject well enough to have a confident opinion.

This is an attempt to learn in public, gather credible sources, and make the tradeoffs easier to examine. Knowledgeable corrections, local experience, better sources, and thoughtful disagreement are welcome.

Chapter 2 · Concern before calculation

The Biggest Concerns

Why should I care, what is the most defensible answer, what remains unknown, and what could reduce the risk?

Generally true, but contract-dependent

Electricity bills and grid costs

Will ordinary customers pay for infrastructure built for a private data center?

Why people are worried: A very large new load can require generation, transmission, and distribution work. The concern is reasonable because someone must pay even if a planned campus is delayed or smaller than announced.

What the evidence supports

They can if costs are broadly allocated or if a promised project is delayed or downsized. Long contracts, minimum bills, collateral, customer-funded facilities, and regulatory cost allocation can reduce that exposure, but the actual tariff and utility order matter more than a developer’s public promise.

Indiana’s utility record shows why headline megawatts are not enough. The IURC’s large-load order addresses contract demand, minimum obligations, collateral, ramp schedules, and risk protections; reporting also documents continuing disagreement about which costs remain shared.

What is established

Large loads may require costly capacity and grid work. Tariffs and contracts can assign some risks to the new customer.

What we still need to know

Requested and contracted load, ramp schedule, minimum billing, collateral, infrastructure allocation, and cancellation or downsizing terms.

What document would answer it?

The applicable utility tariff, filed special contract or facilities agreement, feasibility study, asset-cost schedule, and final regulatory order.

What a responsible agreement would require

Customer-funded dedicated upgrades, binding minimum payments, meaningful collateral, assignment controls, and explicit responsibility for delay, partial buildout, and cancellation.

ConcernHigher bills
EvidenceUtility cost allocation
Missing proofFiled tariff and contract
Protective termMinimum bill, collateral, customer-funded upgrades

Evidence that would reduce concern

  • Fully customer-funded dedicated infrastructure
  • Binding minimum payments and adequate collateral
  • Clear credit for useful shared assets

Evidence that would increase concern

  • Public or shared infrastructure before firm commitments
  • Weak termination security
  • Full-build forecasts without contractual phases

Project- and design-dependent

Water withdrawal and consumption

Is the facility going to drain local water supplies?

Why people are worried: Cooling can create a material new demand, drought risk is local, and a yearly average may hide the summer day when systems are under the most strain.

What the evidence supports

Some cooling designs consume substantial water and others use much less. Annual averages can also conceal peak summer demand. The useful questions are where the water comes from, how much is withdrawn and consumed, what happens during drought, and whether potable or reclaimed water is used.

Withdrawal is water removed from a source; consumption is the portion not promptly available for reuse. A closed recirculating loop can still need makeup water or reject heat through evaporation, so “closed loop” does not mean “zero consumption.”

What is established

Cooling architecture, climate, utilization, and accounting boundaries materially affect water demand.

What we still need to know

Cooling design, annual withdrawal and consumption, peak-day demand, source water, potable and reclaimed shares, drought plan, discharge, and reporting.

What document would answer it?

A phase-by-phase site water balance, utility capacity letter, withdrawal or discharge permit, drought plan, and enforceable public reporting schedule.

What a responsible agreement would require

Annual and peak-day caps, reclaimed-water preference, drought curtailment, source-specific reporting, and consequences for exceeding approved use.

ConcernLocal supply stress
EvidenceWithdrawal and consumption
Missing proofSite water balance
Protective termCaps, drought plan, public reporting

Evidence that would reduce concern

  • Reclaimed-water cooling with verified capacity
  • Low peak-day potable demand
  • Binding drought curtailment

Evidence that would increase concern

  • Potable-water demand without drought protection
  • Only annual averages
  • No disclosed discharge or reporting plan
Site-specific judgment

Farmland conversion

Why pave productive farmland for an industrial project?

Why people are worried: Conversion can be long-lived, affect tenants and drainage beyond the property line, and remove a contiguous block that is more operationally important than its acreage alone suggests.

What the evidence supports

Farmland is not interchangeable. Soil quality, parcel continuity, drainage, tenant operations, nearby infrastructure, and alternative sites matter. A landowner may rationally sell while the county may still rationally reject the proposed use.

Indiana farmland values and rents vary with land quality and region, while agricultural statistics distinguish acreage, tenure, and operation. Those broad sources establish context; parcel suitability still requires soil, drainage, operator, and site evidence.

What is established

Ownership, operation, soil quality, continuity, and drainage are separate considerations.

What we still need to know

Soil classifications, tillable acreage, current operator, drainage, fragmentation, alternative sites, and proximity to existing industrial infrastructure.

What document would answer it?

A soil and agricultural-impact inventory, current operator statement, drainage study, site-selection record, and credible alternative-site analysis.

What a responsible agreement would require

An agricultural siting hierarchy, prime-soil avoidance where practicable, tenant transition, drainage protection, compact phasing, and restoration or decommissioning security.

ConcernPermanent conversion
EvidenceSoils, operator, drainage
Missing proofAlternative-site analysis
Protective termAvoidance, compact siting, restoration

Evidence that would reduce concern

  • An industrially zoned alternative site
  • Lower-quality, fragmented ground near existing infrastructure
  • Protected drainage and remnant access

Evidence that would increase concern

  • High-quality contiguous soils
  • No alternative-site analysis
  • Major tenant or drainage disruption

Model- and design-dependent

Noise, light, generators, and nearby homes

Will nearby residents hear and see this campus around the clock?

Why people are worried: A rural background can be quiet and dark. Continuous mechanical sound, tonal components, intermittent generator tests, and large illuminated buildings can change daily life even when each device meets a generic specification.

What the evidence supports

A server building may appear quiet, but cooling equipment, transformers, pumps, and generators can create persistent or intermittent impacts. Distance, terrain, cumulative buildout, tonal sound, shielding, and enforceable nighttime limits determine the real result.

Mechanical cooling and transformers can create continuous sound; generator testing and emergency operation are intermittent but can be louder and create combustion emissions. EPA maintains separate stationary-engine compliance requirements.

What is established

Different sources have different timing, frequency, and enforcement implications.

What we still need to know

Full-build acoustical model, sensitive receptors, tonal and low-frequency analysis, testing schedule, fixture plan, color temperature, setbacks, and verification.

What document would answer it?

A receptor-specific full-build acoustical study, equipment schedule, photometric plan, generator permit and test plan, and post-construction testing protocol.

What a responsible agreement would require

Nighttime limits at property lines and homes, tonal penalties, testing windows, full-cutoff low-temperature lighting, dimming, buffers, monitoring, correction deadlines, and penalties.

ConcernRound-the-clock impact
EvidenceReceptor model and fixture plan
Missing proofFull-build verification
Protective termLimits, testing windows, shielding

Evidence that would reduce concern

  • Large setbacks and terrain shielding
  • Enforceable property-line and receptor limits
  • Post-construction verification

Evidence that would increase concern

  • No full-build acoustical model
  • Unrestricted generator testing
  • Unshielded or undimmed lighting
Fiscal terms required

Permanent jobs, construction jobs, and tax claims

Is a multibillion-dollar project really worth it if it creates relatively few permanent jobs?

Why people are worried: Construction activity can be large and temporary while permanent staffing is smaller, and the same project may seek exemptions, abatements, TIF support, or public infrastructure.

What the evidence supports

Construction activity, permanent payroll, taxable property, sales-tax exemptions, abatements, infrastructure costs, and partial buildout are different ledgers. A capital-intensive facility can produce substantial revenue with few workers, but announced investment alone does not establish local benefit.

Indiana’s program exempts qualifying equipment and energy from sales and use tax for up to 25 years, or up to 50 years for investments above $750 million. Local governments may make separate property-tax decisions.

What is established

Announced investment, taxable value, temporary work, permanent work, and net local revenue are not interchangeable.

What we still need to know

Construction job-years, permanent FTEs, wages and residency, assessed and taxable value, exemptions, abatements, TIF flows, public costs, phases, and clawbacks.

What document would answer it?

A year-by-year fiscal schedule tied to the assessor’s method, incentive and TIF agreements, infrastructure budgets, payroll commitments, buildout milestones, and enforceable clawbacks.

What a responsible agreement would require

Benefits tied to completed investment and verified employment, phase deadlines, clawbacks, cost reimbursement, annual public reporting, and security for unfinished infrastructure.

ConcernFew jobs, large incentive
EvidenceTax and employment ledgers
Missing proofYear-by-year fiscal schedule
Protective termMilestones, clawbacks, reporting

Evidence that would reduce concern

  • Net-positive public schedule under conservative buildout
  • Completed-investment triggers
  • Road and utility cost reimbursement

Evidence that would increase concern

  • Benefits based only on announced investment
  • Public infrastructure before secured phases
  • No downside case or clawbacks
Stage- and law-dependent

Secrecy, NDAs, and public control

Why should residents trust a project negotiated behind closed doors?

Why people are worried: If important facts arrive after officials are politically or legally committed, a public hearing can become procedural rather than meaningful.

What the evidence supports

Some early confidentiality can protect landowners and active negotiations. It becomes less defensible when the project seeks zoning, tax treatment, utility commitments, or public spending. Disclosure should expand before public decisions become binding.

Indiana’s Open Door Law and Access to Public Records Act—not an NDA alone—govern public meeting and record duties, subject to statutory exceptions. The Public Access Counselor publishes guidance and receives complaints.

Indiana public-access disputes have included requests for data-center NDAs, water-use communications, and redacted utility agreements. That record shows why “there is an NDA” is not a complete legal or civic answer.

What is established

Public-records and open-meeting duties depend on law and specific exceptions, not an NDA by itself.

What we still need to know

NDA scope and parties, claimed exemptions, application, incentive request, utility filings, draft agreement, release schedule, and hearing timeline.

What document would answer it?

The NDA, records-withholding log, land-use application, incentive request, utility filings, draft development agreement, and written release schedule.

What a responsible agreement would require

Disclosure triggers before binding votes, enough review time, public technical studies, narrow written redactions, accessible final agreements, and ongoing compliance reporting.

ConcernDecision made in secret
EvidencePublic-action timeline
Missing proofNDA and release schedule
Protective termDisclosure before binding action

Evidence that would reduce concern

  • Narrow, time-limited confidentiality
  • Draft terms and studies released before hearings
  • Written statutory basis for redactions

Evidence that would increase concern

  • Withheld agreements until after approval
  • Material new facts after public comment
  • NDA invoked instead of legal analysis

Chapter 3 · Private choice and public review

Why This Farm?

Why would a family sell productive farmland?

The concise answer

A landowner compares a liquid offer with annual rent, expenses, debt, risk, taxes, family needs, and attachment to land. A county compares the proposed use with farmland quality, infrastructure exposure, neighbors, alternatives, and enforceable conditions. Those are different decisions, and each can be rational without dictating the other.

USDA’s ownership study found that inheritance and gifts are major paths into nonoperator ownership and that large acreages were expected to transfer through wills, trusts, gifts, and sales. Multiple heirs can therefore face a real liquidity and fairness problem even if the farm is productive.

For an interested successor, desire is not enough: USDA documents financial and land-access constraints facing beginning producers. Buying out siblings at development-offer values may be impossible for a viable operating farm.

What is often misunderstood

A sale does not prove that farming failed, and refusing a sale does not prove the owners ignored economics. High land equity can coexist with modest annual cash flow. A tenant may lose operationally important acres even though the tenant did not control the sale.

Where reasonable disagreement remains

Families can disagree about legacy, diversification, taxes, risk, and fairness among heirs. Counties can disagree about how much high-quality farmland to preserve and whether feasible alternative sites exist.

Chapter 4 · Ownership, operation, margins, and support

Farming Behind the Headlines

Who owns and operates farmland, why is entry difficult, and how do thin margins and public support shape land values?

The concise answer

“Family farm,” “small farm,” “landowner,” and “operator” are not synonyms. A family can own a large operating business and rent thousands of acres from neighbors, retirees, or heirs. High land values can coexist with modest annual cash returns, making inherited land and access to capital especially important.

USDA’s family-farm definition turns on whether the majority of the business is owned by the producer and relatives—not whether the operation is small.

Just over 60% of U.S. land in farms was owner-operated in the 2022 Census. USDA’s tenure research also found that more than half of cropland was rented, especially in grain-producing regions.

Purdue’s 2025 survey reported average-quality Indiana farmland at $12,254 per acre. Land value and annual rent are related but very different measures, so high asset values can coexist with modest cash returns.

What is often misunderstood

Family-owned does not mean small.

Legal form, ownership, operating scale, and buyer power answer different questions.

Rented ground still matters.

Losing one block can affect field efficiency and the whole operation.

Equity is not cash flow.

Appreciation can strengthen a balance sheet without paying annual bills or equalizing heirs.

Farm Support Flow

Crop insurance is subsidized risk protection. ARC responds to specified revenue shortfalls, PLC to prices below an effective reference price, and conservation programs pay for practices or land-use commitments serving environmental goals. They are not interchangeable.

Research reviews generally find that some land-linked agricultural support is capitalized into rents or land prices. That can stabilize production while shifting part of the long-run benefit to landowners and raising barriers for entrants.

Chapter 5 · Facility, requirements, payer, return, downside

The Public Deal

What is being proposed? What does it require? Who pays? What does the public receive? What happens if it is only partly built?

The concise answer

Start with the actual facility and phases, not the “AI” label. Requested capacity is not the same as contracted or average load. Announced investment is not assessed value, taxable value, or local revenue. Electricity exposure is governed by utility tariffs and contracts; fiscal exposure is governed by tax law, incentive agreements, infrastructure commitments, and enforcement.

Berkeley Lab’s national work models facility type, equipment, utilization, climate, and cooling because those variables materially change power and water demand. A generic campus label cannot answer a project-specific impact question.

Indiana’s program exempts qualifying equipment and energy from sales and use tax for up to 25 years, or up to 50 years for investments above $750 million. Local governments may make separate property-tax decisions.

Chapter 6 · Disclosure that grows with public exposure

Transparency and Community Terms

What can remain confidential now, what must be public before a decision, and which promises will be enforceable later?

The concise answer

Early site searches can involve legitimate confidentiality interests: speculative leads, landowner privacy, prices, and proprietary design. Secrecy becomes less defensible when zoning, incentives, utility obligations, or public expenditures are requested. An NDA cannot replace public-records law, and disclosure should expand before residents and officials face a binding choice.

What is often misunderstood

Immediate disclosure of every inquiry can create noise and harm legitimate negotiation. Secrecy until approval can make participation meaningless. Staged transparency ties disclosure to concrete public exposure.

Where reasonable disagreement remains

Trade secrets, security details, negotiating positions, and landowner privacy require judgment under law. The burden is to identify a specific basis, scope, and duration—not to treat all project information as confidential.

Not “no secrets ever,” and not “trust us until the bulldozers arrive.” Confidentiality should narrow as public exposure increases.

Chapter 7 · Evidence before verdict

Evaluate a Proposal

Core question: What is established, what is promised, what remains unknown, and what could make this site a poor fit?

Plain-language explanation

A weighted score can organize judgment, but it cannot turn priorities into objective truth. Strong benefits supported by weak evidence are still weak. A missing utility agreement, water balance, noise model, fiscal schedule, or enforcement mechanism can justify delay. Material unmitigated harm or an unfavorable public deal can justify rejection.

What is often misunderstood

“Insufficient information” is not fence-sitting when the applicant controls missing evidence. Likewise, a low score should identify a site, design, agreement, or enforcement failure—not assume every data center is identical.

Where reasonable disagreement remains

Residents may weight farmland, jobs, tax revenue, climate, infrastructure, neighbor effects, and industrial strategy differently. Make those weights visible and keep the evidence record separate.

Chapter 8 · Provenance and uncertainty

Sources and Method

Core question: Can a reader trace each important factual statement to a usable original source and see its limits?

A note from the creator

I created Ground, Grid and Growth because I kept encountering strong claims about farmland, farming, data centers, water, electricity, and economic development—and realized I did not understand the subject well enough to have a confident opinion.

I am not a farmer, agricultural economist, utility planner, hydrologist, or land-use attorney. This project is an independent attempt to learn in public: to gather credible sources, separate general facts from project-specific questions, and make complicated tradeoffs easier to examine.

That means this work should not be treated as the final word. Farmers, landowners, tenants, agricultural lenders, utility professionals, planners, engineers, public officials, researchers, and affected residents will know things that cannot be learned from datasets and reports alone.

Thoughtful corrections, stronger sources, local examples, and informed disagreement are welcome. The goal is not to protect a predetermined conclusion. It is to make the project more accurate, useful, and fair.

You can also join the companion Facebook group to discuss the project with other readers.

The app treats uncertainty as information. Missing data is displayed as missing data, not silently replaced with a favorable or unfavorable assumption.

Primary government, regulatory, and project records are preferred for rules and measured facts. Academic research is used for causal or economic questions. Industry, developer, advocacy, and journalism sources are not excluded; their institutional perspective and limitations stay visible.

Public concerns and search language change over time. The artifact’s framing is periodically reviewed, while factual claims remain tied to their cited evidence.
