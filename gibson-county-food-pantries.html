From a Facebook question to a useful community tool

This page exists because of a single, ordinary question.

It started with a Facebook question

Someone asked a simple question in a local Facebook community:

“Does anyone have a list of food pantries in Gibson County?”

Traditionally, the answer might be a handful of comments: names, phone numbers, incomplete addresses, and maybe a couple of links. Useful in the moment — but quickly buried in the feed and hard to find again.

What if that question turned into something more permanent and useful?

From question to working application

A question→ Research→ Verification→ Structured data→ Working application→ Community resource

1 Identify the need

A Facebook post reveals a practical community information gap.

2 Gather information

Find listings from food-assistance organizations, municipal websites, Indiana 211, and community resource directories.

3 Verify and reconcile

Compare addresses, phone numbers, operating hours, and service areas. Some listings conflict. Some have not been updated in years. Those differences matter when somebody is trying to get food.

4 Create a structured dataset

Turn the researched information into reusable records with addresses, schedules, eligibility, contact details, sources, and confidence metadata.

5 Build a useful application

Transform the dataset into a searchable directory and an interactive map, with one-tap calls, directions, service-area filters, and source details.

6 Publish and share

Instead of information disappearing into a comment thread, the community gets a page that can be bookmarked, shared, corrected, and improved.

Why this matters

Modern AI-assisted development dramatically reduces the time and repetitive effort involved in building small, focused software tools. A useful local application no longer necessarily requires months of work, an organization with a substantial budget, or a commercial business model.

But automation does not eliminate responsibility. The research still needs to be checked. Conflicting information must be handled. Sources matter. And sometimes the most responsible answer is simply “Call to confirm.”

This project illustrates how a simple Facebook request can become an actionable community resource in short order.

There are thousands of tiny information problems in every community. Most are too small to justify traditional software development, but they are not too small to matter to the people who need the answer.
