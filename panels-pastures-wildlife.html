Panels, Pastures & Wildlife

What can a solar project covering hundreds of acres do to wildlife on farm ground? The answer is neither “nothing” nor “solar kills habitat.” It depends heavily on what habitat is being replaced, what grows beneath and between the panels, how the site is fenced, and how aggressively it is graded and maintained.

The useful question is not “Are solar farms good or bad for wildlife?” It is: What is there now, what will be there afterward, and can animals still feed, nest, and move through the landscape?

The one-minute version

The baseline matters more than the panels

Research increasingly supports a conditional answer: solar can damage good habitat, or it can create a surprisingly useful semi-natural refuge on land that was previously intensively farmed.

Higher concern

If it replaces intact habitat

Clearing forest, native grassland, wetland edges, shrubland, or a known wildlife corridor creates direct habitat loss and fragmentation. Large fenced blocks can sever movement routes for deer and other large mammals.16

Potential upside

If it replaces row-crop monoculture

On intensively farmed land, perennial native vegetation beneath and around panels can add flowering plants, insects, cover, and structural variety. A Minnesota solar-pollinator study found strong gains in flowering-plant richness, insect diversity, native bees, and total insect abundance over four years.3

Design-dependent

If it becomes “panels + gravel + fence”

Much of the ecological opportunity disappears if the site is heavily graded, compacted, graveled, frequently mowed, broadly treated with herbicide, or wrapped in an impermeable fence. DOE research priorities now explicitly include native vegetation, movement corridors, wildlife-permeable fencing, and low-disturbance construction.2

Species by species

What the evidence says about wildlife

“Wildlife” is too broad to have one outcome. Pollinators, grassland birds, forest birds, deer, bats and waterbirds can respond very differently to the same project.

🦌 Deer and other large mammals

Main issue: movement, not the panels themselves. Security fencing can turn a very large site into a barrier. GPS research on pronghorn has documented habitat loss, avoidance and migration disruption around fenced utility-scale solar facilities.6

That western pronghorn research is not a direct white-tailed-deer study, but the barrier mechanism is relevant: a fence is still a fence. For a Midwestern project, the important evidence is the local corridor map and actual fence design.

🐦 Birds

The evidence is mixed by species and setting. Some agricultural-landscape studies find more bird species or individuals at solar sites than nearby crop fields, probably because the sites offer vegetation, insects, perches and nesting opportunities. Other species—especially those tied to forest or unobstructed open habitat—may avoid them.7

Bird collisions and strandings at photovoltaic sites are documented, and USGS has specifically investigated whether some birds respond to reflected polarized light as if panels were water. Attraction does not automatically mean mortality, but the issue is real enough to monitor.5

🐝 Bees, butterflies and other insects

This is where a farm-ground solar project can plausibly produce a real ecological gain. At Minnesota solar sites restored with pollinator habitat, researchers documented increases in floral diversity, insect-group diversity, native bee abundance and total insect abundance; bee visitation to nearby soybeans was also higher near the solar-pollinator habitat than in soybean interiors and roadside locations.3

But that benefit is not automatic. It comes from the seed mix and long-term vegetation management, not from photovoltaic panels by themselves.

🦇 Bats, small mammals, reptiles and amphibians

Long-term, species-specific evidence is thinner. Solar changes shade, temperature, moisture, vegetation height, prey availability and movement paths; different animals can respond in opposite directions. Recent biodiversity reviews emphasize that the evidence base remains uneven across taxa and regions.8

The appropriate response is not to assume “no effect,” but to require a baseline inventory and post-construction monitoring for locally important species.

🌱 Soil, earthworms and the food web

A 2026 study at three Pennsylvania utility-scale sites found that construction caused modest but measurable declines in some soil-health measures, including labile carbon and water-stable aggregates; physical properties showed signs of recovery in the second year. Earthworm abundance was higher inside the solar facilities.4

The practical lesson: how the site is built matters. Topsoil stripping, mass grading and compaction are different ecological disturbances from driving posts into largely undisturbed ground and keeping perennial vegetation.

💧 Drainage, erosion and nearby streams

Panels shed rainfall in concentrated drip lines, and construction equipment can compact soil. Poorly designed sites can create erosion and runoff problems. DOE guidance specifically recommends stormwater design and notes that native/pollinator vegetation can improve infiltration and reduce soil scour.9

For wildlife, that means drainage plans matter beyond the project boundary—especially where runoff reaches ditches, wetlands, ponds or streams.

The key comparison: A fenced solar array should not be compared with some imaginary untouched meadow if the current site is corn and soybeans. It should be compared with the actual present habitat. Likewise, a proposal on native prairie, wetland edge or a wooded corridor should not be waved through because “solar is green.” A 2026 review of 54 biodiversity studies reached essentially that conclusion: impacts tend to be worse when PV replaces natural habitat and can be better on degraded or low-biodiversity land, especially when biodiversity is deliberately built into the project.8

These are the design choices that move a project from “industrial field with some grass” toward a site that still functions as habitat.

Show me, don't tell me

Questions I would put to the developer

For a project covering hundreds of acres, these are reasonable requests before anyone claims the wildlife impacts are either negligible or disastrous.

What is the ecological baseline?Show the current land cover, wetlands, streams, ponds, woodlots, hedgerows, threatened/endangered-species review, and wildlife observations—not just the parcel boundary.
Where are the movement corridors?Show where deer and other terrestrial wildlife currently cross the property and how the fencing/layout preserves or redirects those routes.
Exactly what fence are you proposing?Height, mesh size, ground clearance, total enclosed acreage, gates, and any unfenced corridors or breaks. “Security fencing” is not enough detail.
How much land will actually be disturbed?Ask for acreage to be graded, stripped, compacted, graveled, trenched, roaded or otherwise converted—not simply “project acreage.”
What will grow underneath the panels?Get the seed mix, percentage native, bloom periods, mowing schedule, herbicide/insecticide policy, invasive-species plan and who is responsible for maintaining the vegetation for the life of the project.
What stays untouched?Identify preserved wetlands, drainage ways, tree lines, hedgerows, woods and buffers on the actual site plan.
What is the stormwater plan?Ask how panel drip lines, compacted soils, roads and extreme rain events are modeled—and what monitoring/correction occurs if neighboring fields or waterways see erosion.
What is the bird plan?Ask about nearby wetlands/waterfowl use, overhead lines, nighttime lighting, construction timing, mortality monitoring and reporting.
Will the wildlife plan be enforceable?Native planting, corridor width, mowing restrictions and monitoring should be permit or agreement conditions—not a glossy brochure that can change later.
What happens in 25–35 years?Get the decommissioning bond/financial assurance, removal standard, topsoil/compaction restoration requirements and the party financially responsible if the owner disappears.
If this project is in Indiana: Indiana has a solar-site pollinator habitat scorecard built for Midwestern ecosystems, and Purdue/Indiana planning materials explicitly recognize pollinator-friendly solar and wildlife-friendly fencing as tools local communities can require or encourage.10 Indiana DNR also notes that diverse native pollinator habitat supplies food and cover for insects, birds and small mammals and can improve runoff filtration.12
Fast read

Green flags and red flags

No single item settles the project. But these are useful signals of whether wildlife was designed into the site or bolted onto the presentation.

Encouraging signs

  • Existing row-crop or previously disturbed site
  • Wetlands, woods and hedgerows avoided
  • Minimal grading and preserved topsoil
  • Diverse native/pollinator seed mix
  • Seasonal or limited mowing
  • Mapped wildlife corridors and fence accommodations
  • Low, shielded nighttime lighting
  • Baseline + post-construction monitoring
  • Public reporting and corrective-action triggers
  • Funded decommissioning/restoration obligation

Reasons to dig harder

  • High-quality natural habitat cleared because the parcel is convenient
  • One continuous exclusion fence around hundreds of acres with no movement analysis
  • Mass grading, gravel or bare-ground vegetation control
  • Generic “pollinator-friendly” language without a seed mix or maintenance standard
  • Routine broad-spectrum herbicide or insecticide use
  • Removal of hedgerows, drainage buffers or wetland edges
  • No pre-construction wildlife survey
  • No mortality or habitat monitoring after construction
  • “The land can go back to farming” without measurable soil-restoration standards
  • “Solar is clean energy” offered as the answer to a site-specific ecology question
Facebook-sized answer

A response you can actually post

This keeps the nuance without dropping a research paper into somebody's comment thread.

I dug into this because the wildlife question is more interesting than a simple “solar good / solar bad” answer. The biggest thing I found is that the starting land matters enormously. If a solar project replaces woods, wetlands, native grassland or an important wildlife corridor, habitat loss and fragmentation are legitimate concerns. A few hundred fenced acres can also create a real movement barrier for deer and other larger animals. But if the land is currently intensively farmed corn/soybean ground, a well-designed solar site can actually add habitat compared with what was there before—especially if it uses native grasses and pollinator plants instead of gravel, bare ground or frequently mowed turf. A four-year Minnesota study found substantial increases in flowering plants, insect diversity and native bees at solar sites managed as pollinator habitat. Some studies also find more bird use in solar sites than surrounding crop fields, although the response varies a lot by bird species. So I would not judge the project from “hundreds of acres of panels” alone. I would ask to see the actual wildlife and land-management plan: What habitat is there now? What will be cleared? What kind of fencing? Are wildlife corridors preserved? What grows under the panels? How often will it be mowed or sprayed? What happens to wetlands, hedgerows and drainage? Will wildlife be monitored after construction? And is there a funded decommissioning/soil-restoration plan? In other words, solar arrays absolutely can affect wildlife—but the difference between a bad wildlife project and a surprisingly decent habitat project is often in the siting, fencing, ground cover and maintenance details.
