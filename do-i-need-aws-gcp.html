Hyperscaler necessity audit · evidence dated 2026-10-10

Where AWS and Google Cloud still earn their complexity

This is an investigation, not a manifesto. It asks one question — given GitHub Actions, Cloudflare, a small VPS and a decent local machine, what does a hyperscaler still do better, cheaper, or at all? — and it answers it with capability gates, dated prices and arithmetic you can inspect and audit.

How a conclusion is reached, and where it stops being trustworthy

Method, accuracy contract and self-audit

A recommendation you cannot inspect is a vendor claim with better typography. Everything here is deterministic: hard constraints eliminate first, soft preferences only rank what survives, and every price carries a source, a region, a read date and a confidence.

Audit of Right Tool for the Job · reproduced, classified, adjudicated

Nine disagreements between a written scenario library and its own engine

Right Tool for the Job ships 36 scenarios with written recommendations and a deterministic engine. The engine agreed with the written advice in 27 of 36 cases. This is the audit of the other nine: what actually happened, which side the evidence supports, and what this artifact does differently because of it.

Domain A · where the simpler platforms stop

The capability boundary, drawn from specifications rather than marketing

Hyperscalers are not differentiated by having compute, storage and a database. They are differentiated at the edges of a handful of physical and regulatory limits. This view lists those limits, what it costs to cross one, and what crossing it buys.

Domain B · specialized compute, serverless and batch

Compute: the envelope that decides the question

Memory, disk, wall-clock, accelerator and interconnect are hard limits, not preferences. Compare the families honestly and the platform question usually answers itself before cost is consulted.

Domains C and D · databases and data engineering

Data: D1, Parquet, DuckDB, and the point where a warehouse stops being optional

The most consequential question in this investigation. A single-machine columnar engine over Parquet now does work that required a cluster a decade ago — and at some specific size, concurrency or query shape it stops doing that, which is where managed analytical services come back.

Domain E · object storage, archiving and the fees that hide

Storage: cheap per gigabyte, expensive per surprise

Archival pricing is the most reliably misread column in cloud cost tables. Minimum storage durations, minimum billable object sizes, per-gigabyte retrieval and early-deletion penalties turn a $0.00099/GB-month rate into something else entirely.

Domain F · networking, IAM and the compliance floor

Network, identity and compliance: the part Cloudflare genuinely covers

This is where the received wisdom is most out of date in both directions. Cloudflare has real enterprise controls; AWS and GCP have real enterprise obligations. The only useful question is which specific control you are required to hold, by whom, and in writing.

Domain G · GPUs, managed training, inference and vectors

AI: most problems are inference, and inference rarely needs your own GPU

VRAM, accelerator memory and interconnect are the only AI constraints a hyperscaler unambiguously owns. Everything else — classification, extraction, embedding, batch document processing — is usually an API call with a per-token price and no idle bill.

The signature instrument · does this workload genuinely need AWS or GCP?

The Hyperscaler Necessity Test

Describe the workload. The test eliminates every candidate that violates a hard constraint, prices the survivors, and only then decides whether a hyperscaler is unnecessary, competitive, strongly justified, or forced by a stated constraint. It is allowed to answer that it cannot tell.
