A better equation

AI every day + human expertise when it matters

Use cheap, abundant AI practice where it works. Buy scarce human expertise where it adds the most value.

  • Persistent learning difficulty
  • Specialized expertise
  • Formal assessment
  • Special education expertise
  • Emotional or frustration barriers
  • Accountability
  • Teacher curriculum alignment
  • High-stakes test preparation
  • AI repeatedly fails to explain
Evidence · Information reviewed September 2026

Good tutoring is a design problem.

The chatbot is powerful. The learning pattern wrapped around it determines whether the student practices thinking—or merely watches answers appear.

What learning science already knows

2×+
median learning gain
2025 randomized controlled trial · college physics

A deliberately designed AI tutor beat an active-learning classroom condition on immediate learning.

Students spent a median of 49 minutes with the AI tutor versus about 60 minutes of instruction, while the AI group showed more than twice the median learning gain.

Important: college physics, one engineered tutor, one study. This does not show that ordinary, unstructured chatbot use produces the same result.

The pedagogy wrapped around the AI matters. Scientific Reports, May 2025 ↗

A 2025 systematic review of AI-driven intelligent tutoring systems in K–12 found generally positive outcomes, but advantages were less clear against other tutoring software. The authors call for longer and more diverse studies. npj Science of Learning, May 2025 ↗

EL SALVADOR

Integrate & measure

Encouraging signal, not causal proof
  • 1,198 volunteer students in 171 public schools took PISA for Schools.
  • Reading, math and science results were above El Salvador's national average and comparable to Germany and Sweden's PISA 2022 averages.
  • The broader modernization program includes AI tutoring plus teacher support, materials, assessment, monitoring and technology.
  • The program is expanding.

This was not an experiment isolating AI. It cannot show AI caused the results. It also predates and is distinct from the later nationwide xAI/Grok partnership.

NEW YORK CITY

Pause & restrict

One-year moratorium · 2026–27
  • Student-facing generative AI is barred in grades 2K–8, affecting nearly 600,000 students.
  • High-school use is limited to vetted programs, career readiness and controlled pilots.
  • Every high-school student receives two AI-literacy modules.
  • Teachers retain permitted planning uses; assistive and educational exceptions exist.

NYC's case: protect human interaction, independent reasoning, child development and productive struggle while evidence develops.

The counterargument: supervised, age-appropriate experience can build AI literacy, tutoring access and competency for higher education and work.

NYCPS guidance · 2026 ↗
We favor teaching children how to use powerful tools well over pretending those tools do not exist.

The question isn't whether AI can think for a student. It can. The educational question is whether we can teach students to use AI in ways that make them more capable rather than less capable.

Terms

Parent-language, not lab-language.

Parent guide

Keep the adult in the loop—then widen the lane.

Practical supervision changes with age. School rules still apply, and an AI's confident tone is never proof.

K–5

Parent drives

The parent operates ChatGPT, chooses what to share, and guides the interaction. ChatGPT is not intended for children under 13; for educational use under 13, the actual interaction should be conducted by an adult.

5–8

Parent co-pilot

Use it together. Review prompts and outputs, compare explanations with class materials, and talk about when the chatbot helped versus took over.

9–12

Increasing independence

Ages 13–18 require parental consent. Use age-appropriate account controls and discuss verification, school policies, privacy and responsible submission.

The three-check habit

AI can be confidently wrong.

  1. Important fact?
    Ask where it came from.
  2. Important calculation?
    Check it.
  3. Important assignment?
    Understand what you're submitting.
What shouldn't we paste?

Student names, school IDs, health or disability records, private teacher communications, account credentials, or anything your child would not want retained by a service. Summarize when possible.

Is getting a worked answer cheating?

Not inherently. A worked example can be excellent teaching. The important questions are whether school policy allows the tool, whether the student understands the reasoning, and whether submitted work represents the student's own learning.

When should we stop using the AI?

When frustration rises, explanations keep failing, the subject needs specialist judgment, the child needs human connection, or the AI cannot be verified. A powerful tool is still only one tool.
